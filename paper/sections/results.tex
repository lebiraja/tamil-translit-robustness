\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
All CER values are percentages on the test lexicon unless stated, with mean and standard deviation over five seeds.

\subsection{Clean accuracy and model size}
\Cref{tab:c3} and \Cref{fig:architectures} (left) show clean results.
At 3M parameters the BiLSTM and the Transformer are tied (test CER 9.00 and 9.01; paired-bootstrap difference $-0.01$, 95\% CI $[-0.15,+0.12]$).
At 1M and 8M the BiLSTM is better (by 0.81 and 0.63 CER; both intervals exclude zero), partly because the Transformer is less stable: its 8M model is worse than its 3M model (9.40 against 9.01) with the largest seed spread (sd 0.40, top-1 sd 2.0).
The BiLSTM barely changes between 1M and 8M parameters (8.77 to 9.00), so on this word-level task capacity is not the limit.
Top-5 accuracy is 82--83\% for all models: the right answer is usually in the beam even when it is not first.

\subsection{RQ1: how much does typing noise hurt?}
\label{sec:rq1}
\Cref{fig:noise} shows test CER against severity for the 3M models.
Two operators cause most of the damage.
Vowel dropping raises CER from about 9 to 24 at $p{=}0.3$ (+15.0 for the 1M BiLSTM, +12.6 for the 1M Transformer), and keyboard slips to about 22 (+12.4 to +13.1).
Mixed noise is worst (24--25).
Vowel-length collapse, doubled-consonant collapse and letter stretching cost only 1 to 2 points (+1.0 to +1.1 for V-LEN and GEM, +1.7 to +2.0 for REPEAT), and digraph swaps cost about 3.3.
The damage grows steadily with severity: for MIX the 3M BiLSTM gives 14.8, 20.4, 25.5 and 34.4 at $p=0.1$, $0.2$, $0.3$ and $0.5$.
Keyboard slips were expected to be mild, but they are one of the two most harmful operators; per edited character they hurt more than vowel dropping (KEY edits 7.4\% of characters at $p{=}0.3$ for +12.9 CER, V-DROP 12.7\% for +15.0 CER for the 1M BiLSTM).

\subsection{RQ4: which architecture degrades less?}
\label{sec:rq4}
Under mixed noise at $p{=}0.3$ the Transformer is better at every size (BiLSTM minus Transformer CER: $+0.42$ at 1M, $+1.30$ at 3M, $+0.28$ at 8M; \Cref{tab:stats}), and also degrades less in relative terms (+168\% against +183\% at 3M), most clearly under vowel dropping (21.7 against 24.0 at 3M; \Cref{fig:architectures}).
On clean text the BiLSTM is equal or better.
There is thus a small trade-off, but the gaps are 0.3 to 1.3 CER, of the order of the effects of tuning and seed, so we do not claim that the Transformer is generally more robust.
The item-level bootstrap resamples test items and averages seeds, so its very small $p$ values describe the test set, not the population of trainings.

\subsection{RQ3: which training augmentation helps?}
\label{sec:rq3}
\Cref{tab:c2} and \Cref{fig:augmentation} compare the six conditions for both 3M architectures.
\textbf{Noise training buys robustness and costs clean accuracy.}
Random noise (A1) and Tamil-phonological noise (A2) lower mixed-noise CER by 2.3 and 3.0 for the BiLSTM (1.8 each for the Transformer) but add 0.8 to 0.9 clean CER, more than a 0.5 budget we set in advance.
Part of this cost may come from selecting on clean development CER, which favours the clean model.
\textbf{Tamil-aware noise helps only for what it models.}
A2 beats A1 on vowel dropping (17.7 against 21.7) but is worse on keyboard slips (23.4 against 18.6), which A2 does not model.
For the Transformer A2 and A1 are indistinguishable on mixed noise ($-0.03$, CI $[-0.32,+0.27]$).

\textbf{Extra words, not spelling variety, explain the clean gain of back-transliteration.}
A3 improves clean CER over A0 (8.67 against 9.00 for the BiLSTM), but so does the control with one fixed romanization per word (8.64), and A3 and CTRL are indistinguishable on clean text ($+0.03$, CI $[-0.08,+0.13]$).
The sampled variants add a small robustness gain on mixed noise ($-0.58$ for the BiLSTM, $-0.75$ for the Transformer; \Cref{tab:stats}).
\textbf{The combination is best.}
A4 has the lowest mixed-noise CER for both architectures (21.8) and removes most of A2's clean cost (+0.35 for the BiLSTM, $-0.03$ for the Transformer).
Our design has no ``A2 plus control'' condition, so we cannot say how much of A4's noise gain is due to varied romanizations and how much to the extra words.

\textbf{Robustness does not transfer to a held-out operator.}
Leaving vowel dropping out of A2 removes almost all of its benefit: BiLSTM CER on V-DROP is 24.97 against 17.70 for full A2, which is even slightly worse than the clean baseline A0 (24.03); the Transformer shows the same pattern (22.35 against 17.43 and 21.65; \Cref{fig:heldout}).
Leaving out GEM costs only 0.15 to 0.23 CER on GEM, but GEM is mild to begin with.
A model trained on one error type does not become robust to another, in line with earlier findings~\cite{belinkov2018synthetic}.

\textbf{Independent sets.}
On AK-all, A1 and A2 are worse than A0 (13.7 and 12.7 against 11.7 for the BiLSTM), while A3, CTRL and A4 are better or equal (10.8, 10.7 and 11.3).
The A3 and CTRL words come from the same kind of word list as Aksharantar, so part of that gain may be vocabulary overlap rather than robustness.
Kruskal--Wallis tests over the six conditions are significant for both architectures on clean and mixed noise (effect size $\eta^2=0.88$ to $0.93$), and Dunn's test with Holm correction separates the noise-only conditions (A1, A2) from the data-rich ones (A3, CTRL) on clean text, and A4 from A0 and CTRL on mixed noise; neighbouring conditions such as A2 and A1 for the Transformer are not separated with five seeds.
