\section{Introduction}
Most Tamil speakers type on phones using the Latin alphabet.
A message such as \emph{vanakkam, eppadi irukkinga?} (\tamil{வணக்கம், எப்படி இருக்கிறீர்கள்?}) must be converted to Tamil script for search, autocomplete or display.
This is transliteration: sounds are mapped to letters, not meanings to words.
The Latin alphabet cannot express several Tamil distinctions (vowel length, doubled consonants, and the several n, l and r sounds), so writers spell the same word in many ways; Dakshina annotators produced 2.7 romanizations per word on average~\cite{roark2020dakshina}.
Fast typing adds more variation: dropped vowels (\emph{kankl} for \emph{kanakkil}), shortened long vowels, collapsed double letters, swapped digraphs, stretched letters (\emph{semmaaa}) and key slips~\cite{wolfsonkin2019latin,herath2024tamzhi}.

Neural transliteration is well studied on clean data.
Small character-level BiLSTM and Transformer models reach about 8--9\% character error rate (CER) on the Dakshina Tamil lexicon~\cite{roark2020dakshina,kirov2024context}, and large mined corpora lift accuracy further~\cite{madhani2023aksharantar}.
The Tamil text in these corpora, however, is formal (Wikipedia or mined web text) and was romanized word by word with care.
Evidence from sister languages and from small noisy test sets suggests that clean-trained systems degrade sharply on informal input: Malayalam CER rises from 7.4 to 22.7 when vowels are dropped~\cite{baiju2024malayalam}, and every system, including large language models, falls to about 20\% top-1 on 100 hand-noised Tamil words~\cite{azam2025beyond}.
What is missing is a \emph{controlled} measurement for Tamil: paired clean and noisy versions of the same test words, per-phenomenon breakdowns, severity levels, and a comparison of training remedies and architectures under the same noise.

The architecture question is also open.
On Dakshina Tamil an LSTM was better than a Transformer in one study~\cite{roark2020dakshina} and worse in another~\cite{kirov2024context}; Transformers depend on batch size~\cite{wu2021applying} and often win only with more parameters~\cite{moran2020effective}.
No comparison has tested noisy input.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(1)~A paired, seeded robustness benchmark for Tamil: seven noise operators at four severities applied to the 6{,}833 distinct Dakshina test inputs, with the realised edit rate of every set reported.
(2)~An augmentation study with six training conditions and two held-out-operator variants, tested on every noise type, on independent Aksharantar sets and against a more-data control that separates ``more words'' from ``more spelling variety''.
(3)~A parameter-matched, equal-tuning (20 random-search trials) comparison of BiLSTM-attention and Transformer at three sizes with five seeds, paired bootstrap and Kruskal--Wallis tests.
(4)~A reproducibility package with code, configurations, per-run logs and data checksums, and a deployed single-file model with a typing demo.

\textbf{Findings in brief.}
Vowel dropping and keyboard slips cause most of the damage (\Cref{sec:rq1}).
Tamil-aware noise training removes most of the vowel-drop damage but only for the operators it models (\Cref{sec:rq3}).
Extra romanized words help clean accuracy as much as sampled spelling variants do, and the variants add a small robustness gain.
The two architectures are close, with the Transformer slightly more robust to heavy mixed noise (\Cref{sec:rq4}).
We do not yet evaluate real chat typing, so the link between synthetic and real noise remains open (\Cref{sec:limits}).
